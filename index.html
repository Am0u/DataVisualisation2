Where did fire strike?

A Nation Scorched

The 2019–20 bushfire season — dubbed "Black Summer" — was unprecedented in scale. New South Wales alone lost more land to fire than the total area of Ireland. The map below shows how severely each state was affected.

Has it always been this bad?

The Burning Gets Worse

Australian bushfires are natural. But the scale and severity of recent seasons is dramatically beyond historical norms. The 2019–20 season burned more than seven times the annual average for south-eastern Australia.

Long-term trend · Chart 2
Annual Forest Area Burnt — South-Eastern Australia, 1999 to 2021 (Thousand Hectares)

Source: ABARES State of the Forests Reports (various years). Covers NSW, VIC, SA, ACT and TAS. Figures are approximate estimates combining multiple ABARES reports.

Why Black Summer Was Different

Australian forests have always burned. But three factors converged in 2019–20 to create a catastrophe of a different order: record heat (Australia's hottest year on record at +1.52°C above average), severe drought (following three years of below-average rainfall), and an early start (fire season began in August, three months before its historical norm) The result was fires that could not be controlled, and a season that burned for over 240 days.

Seasonal patterns

When Does Fire Strike?

Fire danger in Australia peaks at different times depending on the region. Southern states face extreme fire risk in summer; northern Queensland and Western Australia face elevated danger in the dry season (July–October). Understanding these patterns helps communities prepare.

The human and ecological toll

More Than Statistics

Behind the hectare figures are real homes, communities, and ecosystems. Black Summer directly killed 33 people and destroyed over 3,000 buildings. Beyond the built environment, ecologists estimated that 3 billion animals were killed or displaced — one of the largest single-event wildlife disasters ever recorded.

The climate connection

A Warming Continent

Australia has warmed by approximately 1.4°C since 1910. Rising temperatures directly increase fire danger: they dry out fuels faster, extend the fire season, and create conditions where fires burn hotter and spread further. The connection between warming and fire risk is clear — and growing.

Seven decades of warming · Chart 9
Australian Temperature Anomaly, Year by Year (1950–2023)

Source: Bureau of Meteorology, Annual Climate Summaries (1950–2023). Each vertical stripe represents one year, coloured by its mean temperature anomaly versus the 1961–1990 baseline — blue = cooler, red = hotter. The shift from predominantly cool stripes in the mid-20th century to a wall of red in the 21st makes the warming trajectory legible at a glance.

Recent detail · Chart 10

Source: Bureau of Meteorology, Annual Climate Summaries. Bars above the zero line indicate warmer-than-average years. The dashed trendline shows a clear and statistically significant warming trajectory. 2019 remains one of Australia's two hottest years on record.

Climate–fire correlation · Chart 11
